A new program being developed at MAPS · Greater Seattle

Twelve weeks to turn a real problem into a working venture.

MAPS Combinator is a selective, equity-free incubator for early-stage teams in Greater Seattle. Participants validate a problem that matters, build and test a useful product with real users, and leave with a clear next-stage decision — creating ventures that serve the communities around them.

Why it matters here

A program that pays its way back to Greater Seattle.

The point of MAPS Combinator is not startups for their own sake. It is a repeatable way for this region to turn local problems, local talent, and local mentorship into useful ventures — and to make each cohort strengthen the community that hosts it.

Ventures built on local problems

Every team must identify a specific affected customer and test with real users — starting with the people, businesses, and institutions of Greater Seattle. The program's output is ventures that solve problems people here actually have.

Practical skills that stay local

Participants learn customer discovery, product building, testing, and basic operations by doing. That capability remains in the region whether or not a given venture continues.

A working mentorship loop

Local operators, engineers, and founders contribute office hours during the cohort. Alumni are expected to return as mentors, employers, pilot partners, or sponsors for the cohorts after them.

Honest standards, no hype

Equity-free, evidence-driven, and ethically screened. Ventures are judged on real user behavior and community benefit — not pitch polish. Teams whose ideas do not hold up are helped to a responsible stop, not paraded.

How it works

Six pieces of work. One honest decision.

  1. 01

    Define the problem and beachhead customer

    Identify a specific affected person, the context in which the problem occurs, existing alternatives, urgency, and the reason a new solution may deserve adoption.

  2. 02

    Replace assumptions with evidence

    Conduct direct discovery, observe behavior, test willingness to act or pay, and record what changed in the team's understanding.

  3. 03

    Build the smallest useful product

    Create an artifact that can generate learning: a prototype, workflow, service, pilot, technical proof, or working product.

  4. 04

    Test adoption and distribution

    Put the product in front of real users. Test activation, repeat use, pricing, referrals, purchasing, partnerships, or another behavior appropriate to the venture.

  5. 05

    Build an operating foundation

    Learn the basics of team agreements, ownership, intellectual property, incorporation choices, finance, responsible data use, and risk.

  6. 06

    Make a clear next-stage decision

    At Demo Day, teams present the problem, evidence, product, learning, and next step — growth, an external accelerator, continued bootstrapping, a pivot, or a responsible stop.

Who it is for

The pilot is designed for university seniors, recent graduates, early-career professionals, and other serious early-stage builders in Greater Seattle. Applicants may be technical or nontechnical, solo or part of a team — but they must show evidence of action and the capacity to meet the cohort commitment.

Evidence of action

Ideas and enthusiasm alone are not enough. Strong applications include a relevant artifact: a prototype, customer interviews, early usage, a service delivered manually, preorders, letters of intent, a technical experiment, or another credible sign that the applicant has begun learning through action.

Beyond founders

The community makes the program work.

Every cohort needs three things from Greater Seattle: experienced people willing to hold office hours, organizations willing to be honest first customers or pilot partners, and sponsors who fund specific, measurable program needs.
